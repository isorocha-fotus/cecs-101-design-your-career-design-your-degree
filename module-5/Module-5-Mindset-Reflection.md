# Module 5: Uncertainty and Revision Mindset Reflection

Before you can productively test your plan, you need to examine your relationship with uncertainty and revision. This activity helps you identify and articulate your current mindset so you can approach the rest of this module with honesty and openness.

---

**1. When you think about the career and degree plan you have built across Modules 2, 3, and 4, what part of it feels most solid and well-grounded? Why?**

The most solid part of my plan is my degree path itself. I built my four-year plan directly from my degree audit, so I know exactly where I stand: all of my general education requirements are done, I have 91 credit hours and a 3.64 GPA, and I can see which courses are left before I graduate in Fall 2027. My choice of Applied AI as a major also feels well-grounded, because my RIASEC results, my career research, and my career vision all pointed toward building technology. It's not just a plan on paper, either. I'm already building websites and AI tools for clients and developing products for my own brand, so I know I actually enjoy this work.

**2. What part of your plan feels most uncertain or fragile — the part you are least confident about? Be specific.**

The most fragile part is Spring and Summer 2027. My plan has me taking three core AI courses (AI 202, 301, and 302) plus CHEM 260 and microbiology for my Food Science minor in the same semester, and I don't know yet whether that load is realistic. My plan also depends on getting a summer internship for CECS 375, which is required for my degree, and I haven't applied anywhere yet. If I don't land one, the internship moves to my final semester and Spring becomes 18 hours, which could put my Fall 2027 graduation at risk. Beyond graduation, I'm also honestly unsure whether I'll go full-time with my own company or take a job first.

**3. How do you typically respond when a plan you have worked hard on turns out to need significant revision? Do you resist the change, adapt quickly, or something in between? Where does that tendency come from?**

I'm somewhere in between, but closer to adapting quickly. When I realized food science alone wasn't the right fit, I switched into Applied AI as a senior instead of staying on a path that didn't feel right. Running my own brand has also made me used to changing course, because a formula that fails or a cost that comes in too high means you have to rethink and try again. Where I resist is in letting things go. When a plan needs to change, I tend to add the new thing on top instead of dropping something, which is why my schedule gets overloaded. I think that comes from not wanting to waste the work I've already put in, like the food science courses behind my minor.

**4. On a scale of 1–10, how flexible do you consider your current career-degree plan to be? (1 = completely rigid, 10 = completely open.) What would need to change for that number to go up?**

I would rate my plan a 6. The degree part is fairly rigid because I'm a senior with a tight timeline, so there isn't much room to move courses around without delaying graduation. The career part is much more open, since my research showed that I could see myself as an AI developer, a sales engineer, a food product developer, or running my own company. For the number to go up, I would need to secure an internship early, confirm with my advisor which courses are truly required, and decide whether I would be willing to drop the Food Science minor if it threatened my graduation date. Having a real Plan B for the internship would make the whole plan more flexible.

**5. What is one assumption embedded in your plan — something you have been treating as certain — that you have never seriously questioned?**

My career vision assumes that small food and beverage producers want software tools for costing, formulation, and scaling, and that they would pay for them. I've been treating that as a fact because it's a problem I run into with my own brand, but I've never actually asked other small producers whether they feel the same way. They might already have tools that work, or they might not have the budget or the interest. Before building my career around this idea, I need to test it by talking to real producers, which is exactly what this module is about.
