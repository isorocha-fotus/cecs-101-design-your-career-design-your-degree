# Pitch It From the Inside: An Intrapreneurial Opportunity Proposal

## Purpose

You don't need to start a company to be an entrepreneur. Some of the most valuable ideas in any organization come from people early in their careers who notice something that isn't working and take the initiative to fix it. That's **intrapreneurship**: acting like an entrepreneur from inside an existing organization. In this assignment you'll find a real problem or untapped opportunity in an organization you know, design a small way to test a solution, and pitch it the way you would to an actual manager.

This is not a business plan. It's an exercise in noticing problems, building a case with evidence, and earning buy-in when you don't have formal authority, which is the real challenge intrapreneurs face early in their careers.

---

## Instructions

### Step 1: Choose Your Organization

Pick an organization you have real access to and know well enough to spot problems in. Choose one:

- A current or past job, full-time or part-time
- An internship or co-op
- A campus office, department, or lab
- A student organization or club you belong to
- A volunteer group, nonprofit, or religious organization
- Your own option, approved by the instructor

Real access matters. You'll need to observe how things work and talk to at least two or three people involved, so choose somewhere you can actually do that.

### Step 2: Find the Opportunity

Identify one specific problem or opportunity inside that organization. It can be small. Good intrapreneurial ideas often start with a frustrating process, a repeated complaint, wasted time or money, or a customer or member need no one is addressing.

To make sure the problem is real and not just your opinion, gather evidence:

1. Observe the problem firsthand and write down what you see
2. Talk to at least **2–3 people** affected by it, such as coworkers, members, customers, or users
3. Look for any existing data the organization already has, such as complaints, wait times, sign-up numbers, or survey results

Keep notes from your conversations. You'll use them in your presentation.

### Step 3: Build Your Proposal

This is the core of the assignment. Develop your proposal by answering the following:

**A. What is the problem, and how do you know it's real?** Describe the problem or opportunity in plain language. Who does it affect, how often, and what does it cost the organization in time, money, satisfaction, or missed chances? Back it up with what you observed and heard in Step 2.

**B. What's your solution, and how would you test it small?** Describe your idea. Then design one **low-cost experiment** you could realistically run within your current role and resources, before asking anyone for a big budget or approval. What would you try, for how long, and with whom?

**C. Who needs to say yes?** Create a **stakeholder map** for your idea:

- Who has the authority to approve it?
- Who would be affected by the change, positively or negatively?
- Who might resist it, and why? Think about workload, habits, budget, or turf.
- What's in it for each person? How would you frame the idea so they care?

**D. What's the impact, and how would you measure it?** Explain the expected benefits in terms the organization cares about, such as time saved, cost reduced, revenue gained, satisfaction improved, or risk lowered. Then name at least one specific way you'd measure whether your test worked.

**E. Reflect on the reality.** What would make this idea hard to push forward in your organization? What skills or relationships would you need to build to succeed? And if your manager said "no," what would you do next?

## Deliverable

A 5-minute in-class presentation, pitched as if you're speaking to a manager or decision-maker at your organization, covering:

1. Your organization and the problem or opportunity you found
2. The evidence that shows the problem is real
3. Your proposed solution and small-scale test
4. Your stakeholder map and strategy for getting buy-in
5. The expected impact and how you'd measure success
6. Your reflection on the challenges and what you'd do if the answer is "no"

Be ready to answer questions from classmates and the instructor, who will play the role of a skeptical manager.

Record your presentation and upload it to YouTube. You can set it to public or unlisted, whichever you prefer, as long as the link is accessible to the instructor.
