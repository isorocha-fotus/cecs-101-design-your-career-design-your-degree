# Career Exploration Map Activity

This activity asks you to research at least three careers in depth using professional tools. You will build a Career Exploration Map — a structured, comparative overview of the careers you find most compelling. This becomes a reference document you will use in Section 4.5 when building your Career Plan.

---

## Step 1 — Select Three Careers to Research

Based on your RIASEC profile, your self-assessment, and your Ideation Intentions, identify three careers you want to explore in depth.

💡 **Guidance on selection:** Choose careers that genuinely interest you — even if you are uncertain about them.
- One should be a career you have considered seriously.
- One can be a career that surprised you in your RIASEC results.
- One can be a career that feels like a stretch but has always intrigued you.

My Holland Code is **E-I-C** (Enterprising, Investigative, Conventional). I chose one career from each angle:

**Career 1:** AI Applications Developer (Software Developer). This is the one I've considered most seriously, and it's what my Applied AI degree prepares me for.
**Career 2:** Sales Engineer. This one surprised me in my RIASEC results. It's coded Enterprising, my strongest type, and it combines technical knowledge with selling.
**Career 3:** Food Scientist / Product Development Scientist. This is the stretch. It's what I already do for my own brand, but doing it professionally would take more science than my minor gives me.

**What people in this career actually do day-to-day:**
*(Summarize the core tasks and responsibilities — in your own words.)*
They figure out what users need and then build software to do it. For an AI-focused developer, that means things like chatbots, automation tools, or apps that use AI models. A normal day is a mix of writing and testing code, fixing bugs, meeting with the team or clients to decide what to build next, and documenting how things work. **Work environment:**

**Source(s) used:**
- [x] O*NET: [Sales Engineers, 41-9031.00](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/41-9031.00)

- [ ] LinkedIn

**What people in this career actually do day-to-day:**
*(Summarize the core tasks and responsibilities — in your own words.)*
A sales engineer is the technical expert on a sales team. They learn what a customer actually needs, give demos and technical presentations, put together proposals, answer the hard technical questions, and help close the deal. After the sale, they help with setup and troubleshooting. They sell products that need real technical knowledge to explain, such as software, AI platforms, or specialized equipment.

**Work environment:**
*(Where do they work? Office? Field? Hospital? Remote? With people? Alone? Physical? Sedentary?)*
A mix of office work and travel to meet customers, sometimes several days a month away from home. It's very people-facing. According to the BLS, most work full time and some work more than 40 hours, and pay and job security can depend on how much they sell, which can be stressful. The largest employers are durable goods wholesalers (22%), manufacturing (21%), computer systems design (18%), and software publishers (6%).

**Required education and training:**
*(What degree level is typically required? Any certifications, licenses, or graduate degrees needed?)*
Usually a bachelor's degree in engineering or a related technical field. Some come from business backgrounds and add technical training. New hires get on-the-job training on the company's products. No license is required.

**Key skills and abilities required:**

## Career 3: Food Scientist / Product Development Scientist

**Source(s) used:**
- [x] O*NET: [Food Scientists and Technologists, 19-1012.00](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/19-1012.00)
- [x] BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook: [Agricultural and Food Scientists](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/life-physical-and-social-science/agricultural-and-food-scientists.htm)
- [ ] LinkedIn
- [ ] Other

**What people in this career actually do day-to-day:**
*(Summarize the core tasks and responsibilities — in your own words.)*
A product development scientist creates new foods and drinks and improves existing ones. Day to day, they make and test small batches, adjust formulas to get the right flavor, texture, and shelf life, run taste tests, check that ingredients and finished products are safe, and write product specifications. They also make sure products meet FDA and USDA rules and work with manufacturing to scale a recipe from the lab to a production line.

**Work environment:**
*(Where do they work? Office? Field? Hospital? Remote? With people? Alone? Physical? Sedentary?)*
A mix of labs, test kitchens, offices, and processing plants. It's more hands-on and physical than the other two careers. According to the BLS, production facilities can be noisy and hot or cold, and some jobs require protective equipment or travel. For agricultural and food scientists overall, the largest employers are food manufacturing (18%), research and development firms (10%), universities (10%), crop production (9%), and government (9%).

**Required education and training:**
*(What degree level is typically required? Any certifications, licenses, or graduate degrees needed?)*
A bachelor's degree in food science or a related science. According to the BLS, some employers prefer a master's or Ph.D., especially for research roles. IFT's Certified Food Scientist (CFS) credential is optional but can help with advancement. Internships are highly recommended.

**Key skills and abilities required:**
*(List the top 5 skills O*NET identifies for this career.)*
1. Science
2. Critical Thinking
3. Active Learning
4. Quality Control Analysis
5. Complex Problem Solving

**Salary range:**

- Experienced: more than $142,300 (highest 10%, all agricultural and food scientists)

*(BLS, May 2025. The BLS only publishes the entry and experienced figures for the overall occupation group.)*
